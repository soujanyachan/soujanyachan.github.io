I've made a handful myself. I have achieved several rather ugly ones that work well, and that's a point of pride. Of course, I also made some that didn't work and fell apart when I first tried. 

Here is your guide to western style teapot mechanics. Forgive the length. I got going.

It is challenging if you're new to using teapots, because my first advice would be that if you have (or a friend has) one that works well, ignore basic appearance, and use it as a practice model to look closely at things like:

 * the height of the spout compared to the top of the pot (you can never fill it past the height of the top of the spout, no matter how tall the body of the pot is).

 * the way the lid sits into/onto the pot. When you pour near-boiling or boiling water into the pot, there will be an instant wave of steam that continues through the steeping process. If the pot isn't designed to cope, you can either have steam condensing on the underside/edge of the lid and dripping down the outside of the pot while the tea steeps, or you can have pressure build up from the steam and push liquid out of the spout as the steam expands inside the pot. If you look at most (good) traditional ceramic teapots, you will see either a raised lip/flange/ring that the lid sits down _into_ (_not_ on top of), or a tiny steam hole in the lid. This teapot does both things: https://image.invaluable.com/housePhotos/onesourceauctions/42/669942/H4367-L206268037_original.jpg  

 * You may also see a teapot lid that is slightly recessed into the top of the teapot, where the lid has a deep flange made onto the bottom of it, to allow steam to drip back down inside the pot. Look at this one with a deep flange https://www.blueandwhite.com/images/products/BNWW99910LF.jpg  compared to this one without https://www.blueandwhite.com/images/products/bnrb99910lF.jpg  The one without can work well if there is a steam hole or if it sits deeply into a flange made onto the top of the pot like the green pot in the first example above.

 * Also look at whether the lid is easy to take on and off. It shouldn't fit super tight, as that can cause a steam/heat seal and get stuck when hot, or make steam trapping worse. 

 * for your ease and convenience, a little bump sticking out of the edge of the lid flange can keep it from falling off easily when you pour (like on our deep flange lid example above).

 * When you look at the pot, pick it up and go through the motions as if you were pouring tea out. Does it feel a lot heavier on one side? That's bad. Does it seem to be uncomfortably heavy already? It will be a lot heavier when filled with liquid. 

 * Also consider the handle. It doesn't really matter if it's over the top or on the side. It matters if it is substantial enough to "handle" the weight of the pot. In a hand made pottery, if the pot is heavy, chunky, or thick, but the handle is thin and delicate, that's a mismatch, and makes it harder to pour and the handle more likely to break off with normal use. Also look at the handle especially closely for any sign or lines/cracks (even hair thin) or bubbles, because those are signs of poor construction or firing. You can have a delicate handle if you have a delicate, small, thin pot that corresponds. Buy the teapot with the thin delicate handle from the old potter who loves tea.

 * The spout, other than height, should have a tip that has the bottom edge further from the body than the top edge. The end of the spout, no matter the exact shape, should be smooth, not sharp, and no bubbles in the glaze. Spout example one https://i.pinimg.com/736x/aa/34/c7/aa34c702f5662454b0d64f81c8e58f79.jpg   Spout example two https://www.christies.com/img/LotImages/2016/HGK/2016_HGK_12563_3341_000(a_yixing_teapot_late_qing_dynasty).jpg and spout example three https://twgtea.com/Files/Images/TWG-Tea/Products/detailzoom1200x900/A1600Y.jpg are all different ways to achieve the same end. 

 * Always check the inside as much as possible for even glaze coverage with no bubbles or cracks. Even the inside of the spout must be well glazes to stand up to tea making and serving. Any spot missing glaze can soak up liquid over time, leading to cracked pottery.

 * Don't buy a teapot that looks in any way "experimental" unless you can try out pouring water from it, or you just enjoy it as sculpture. I wouldn't buy this expecting to use it without trying the pour https://c1.staticflickr.com/3/2248/1750283816_30a21e861d_b.jpg  or knowing the potter very well and trusting their ability/intent. This one, I'd buy in a heartbeat (if the glazing was good inside) https://www.iauctionshop.co.uk/image/cache/catalog/fast_upload/2018/8/7/denby-chiltern-teapot-4-500x500.jpg

I hope that helps. I know it was a lot. There will always be exceptions, as there's nothing like holding the pot and pouring liquid from it, and _some_ potters are very clever and able to make a statue that would brew an emperor worthy cup, but these are good design functional basics I've observed.
